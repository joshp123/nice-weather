How Nice Weather works

Nice Weather answers one question: where on earth, and when, is the weather nice? Nice here means warm enough for a t-shirt and shorts from morning until you walk home in the evening, without rain, muggy air or a gale. This page explains exactly how each day is scored, where the weather data comes from, and where we know it is wrong, so you can check it.

A day's score

Every day gets a score from 0 to 100, from the hourly weather between 10:00 and 21:00 local time.

Three temperature grades decide how much each hour counts. Four other curves decide the share of the day kept. These charts show the default taste. Move the sliders on the map to change it.

Warmth in the sun

How warm it feels where you would stand on a cool day. We use apparent temperature: air temperature adjusted for wind, humidity and sunlight.

Each daylight hour from 10:00 until sunset, at most until 20:00, gets a grade. At 23 °C it counts in full. At 17 °C it counts 40%; at 9 °C it counts nothing.

Heat in the shade

How hot it feels where you would go on a hot day. Each hour gets the lower of its warmth and heat grades.

Up to 28 °C feels-like keeps full marks. At 34 °C it keeps 60; at 43 °C it keeps none. Dry heat feels cooler than humid heat at the same air temperature.

The walk home at 21:00

How warm it feels at 21:00 local time, without the sun. Walking keeps you warm, so the bar is lower: 19 °C gets full marks, 12 °C gets 65.

It counts like two daytime hours. The heat curve still applies, so an oppressively hot evening scores less. Warmth is the average of these hours and the walk home.

Rain

The total rain from 10:00 to 21:00, in millimetres. It reduces the share of the day kept: 1 mm keeps 88%, 4 mm keeps half, 8 mm keeps 22%.

Wind

The mean wind speed from 10:00 to 21:00, in km/h. Gusts run higher. Wind already affects the felt temperature; this curve covers the nuisance of wind itself.

Up to 15 km/h keeps the day in full. At 30 km/h it keeps 85%; at 46 km/h it keeps half.

Humidity

The mean dew point from 10:00 to 21:00, in °C. Dew point measures moisture in the air. A higher value feels muggier.

Up to 16 °C keeps the day in full. At 20 °C it keeps 85%; at 23 °C about half; at 26 °C a fifth.

Sunshine

The share of daylight hours from 10:00 with strong direct sunlight. An hour counts as sunny at 300 W/m² of direct sunlight or more.

Sunshine in at least 80% of these hours keeps full marks. A completely grey day keeps 75%. Sunshine has no taste slider.

The numbered dots are monthly means of the eight representative real days in each place's nearest 1° land square, from the published weather files. They show the conditions in that square, before the finer temperature correction. Warmth uses 14:00 in the sun; heat uses 15:00 in the shade. A curve's value at the mean is a reference, not that month's average score.

The final score is warmth multiplied by the four shares kept. For example, warmth of 80 with rain keeping half becomes 40 before wind, humidity and sunshine count.

The six words on the map follow the score: Awful under 25, Poor from 25, Fair from 40, Nice from 55, Beautiful from 70, Perfect from 85.

For a stretch of days, the map describes the typical day: the middle of the daily scores, with half the days reaching at least that band. So “Perfect most days” means at least half the days score 85 or more. Each month counts its real number of days, so a short February does not count as much as a long January. You can choose one month, any run of months, the whole year or the next 14 days. One forecast day keeps its own score.

The place card also counts the days: nice or better (55 or more), perfect (85 or more) and awful (under 25). Perfect days are included in nice or better. The typical year uses eight representative real days per month, weighted to that month's length, so these counts are estimates rounded to whole days. Forecast counts use each day's score.

Checking it against places people know

Useful checks include humid Singapore and Bangkok, northern Europe in winter, and dry winter escapes such as Cape Town and the Canary Islands. The rule aims for warm, sunny, dry and calm weather. It does not measure how good a place is to visit.

The weather data

  • The typical year comes from ERA5, the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts' record of past weather, hour by hour, read from Earthmover's free public copy. It uses real days from __YEARS__. More years are being added.
  • The next 14 days come from the same centre's free forecast, refreshed every morning. Its free version has no direct-sunlight field, so sunshine is estimated from total solar radiation against a clear sky.
  • Detail. The weather is stored for squares of 1° (about 100 km). Temperature then gets finer detail on a 25 km grid: each point is shifted by how much warmer or cooler it runs than its square, month by month. Coastal towns sit near sea level but their grid square's average height includes the hills behind them, so coastal points take their temperature from the nearest mostly-land square and correct it to sea level (6.5 °C per km, at most 600 m). This helps separate coastal towns from the hills behind them.
  • Fast sliders. For each square and month, eight real days represent the month's range of weather. Your browser re-scores them on its graphics chip when you move a slider.
  • Place names come from GeoNames; the base map from OpenFreeMap and OpenStreetMap.

Where it is wrong

  • A 25 km grid misses microclimates. San Francisco's fog belt, a sheltered bay, a single valley: anything smaller than the grid reads like its surroundings. San Francisco's summer coast can read too warm and too nice. A finer temperature correction is being tested.
  • Fog and sea mist hide the sun, and the data often misses that, so foggy coasts can read too nice.
  • Drizzle. ERA5 can count too many light-rain days, so dry places may read wetter than they are.
  • A few years are not a climate. The typical year uses __YEAR_COUNT__ of real weather so far. This is a sample of recent weather, not a standard 30-year climate average. More years reduce the influence of unusual seasons.
  • The forecast's sunshine is estimated, so a forecast day's sun share is rougher than the typical year's.
