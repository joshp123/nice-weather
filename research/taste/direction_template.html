What the sliders mean

T-shirt weather
An hour counts when it feels at least the lower figure in the sun and at most the upper one in the shade, as you'd choose yourself. Colder doesn't count at all. Hotter fades out over 3 °C, because a bit too hot is easier to live with than a bit too cold. Only daylight hours up to 20:00 count; after dark, the walk home decides. For scale: Málaga at 13:00 in November feels 22 °C in the sun; Seville at 16:00 in July, 35 °C in the shade.
The walk home at 21:00
Colder, and it isn't a nice day. Walking warms you, so a t-shirt stays fine a few degrees cooler than sitting still. For scale: San Diego at 21:00 feels 11 °C in January (13 °C on the thermometer) and 25 °C in August; Málaga feels 14 °C in November.
Humidity
The day's average dew point, 10:00 to 21:00. More humid, and it isn't a nice day. Dew point measures the moisture in the air as a temperature: the higher, the muggier. Humidity in % is no use here: it falls as the day warms while the air stays just as muggy. For scale: Seville in July 15 °C, San Diego and Málaga in August 18 °C, Austin in July 21 °C, Singapore 24 °C all year.
Rain
Rain from 10:00 to 21:00. Wetter, and it isn't a nice day. Weather services call 1 mm or more a wet day: a short shower, or a couple of hours of drizzle. 5 mm is a few proper showers; 10 mm, a wet afternoon.
Wind
The day's average wind, 10:00 to 21:00; gusts run higher. Windier, and it isn't a nice day. Wind already counts in how warm it feels, so this limit is only for the nuisance: dust, sand, hair. For scale: Cape Town in January averages 25 km/h, and over 35 km/h on about one day in eight.
Feels like
The thermometer adjusted for humidity, wind and sun, by the formula Australia's weather service uses (Steadman's apparent temperature). On a calm, dry night it reads 2 to 3 °C below the thermometer; on a humid afternoon, several degrees above.
Grades
The forecast grades each day by its share of t-shirt hours: 55% or more is Nice, 70% Beautiful, 85% Perfect; below that, Fair, Poor or Awful. A day that breaks a limit is Fair at best. The table counts Nice or better.

How the map keeps up with the sliders

Under 0.2 s for any change, anywhere on the map. Every 100 km square keeps a small sample of real days. When a slider moves, your device's graphics chip recounts them and adds the detail for height and coast pixel by pixel, the way Theo's map does. Rain, wind and humidity change slowly over distance, so 100 km is enough for them; temperature, which changes with every hill and coast, gets the pixel detail. Nothing waits on a server while you drag. Sites does the heavy work in advance: it builds the samples, and the map at your default taste in full detail, every day, so the first view is instant.

The small day summary this needs gives the same answer as the full hour-by-hour calculation, __COMPACT_CHECK__. This page runs exactly that recount. The timing for the whole world on a phone is the first thing I measure when building.

What I'm fixing anyway

  • Coasts. Today coastal towns read 2 to 4 °C too cold, because each square mixes sea, coast and mountain. Taking temperature from the nearest land and correcting for height cuts the error to under 1 °C against weather stations. Cape Town still reads about 3 °C too hot in summer; that gets fixed before it goes live.
  • Rain. The data counts too many wet days in dry places: Málaga in January reads wet on 26% of days, its weather station 16.5%. I'll test Theo's correction against stations and keep whatever matches them.
  • Forecast and climate agree. Both use the same sun measure (from solar radiation) and the forecast adds gusts. 15 forecast days, as ECMWF gives; ECMWF's 9 km forecast when it becomes free.
  • The 20-year climate is built on Sites. It works; run one area at a time, the world would take weeks, so it runs in parallel.
